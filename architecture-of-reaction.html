The approach

Rethinking the foundations of acting.

Before the emotion,
there is a response.

Acting techniques have been passed down through generations. Yet actor training has often lagged behind advances in psychology and neuroscience. The Architecture of Reaction revisits familiar practices in the light of contemporary ideas about human behaviour.

Rather than demonstrating emotion, actors investigate how perception, memory, conditioning, bodily sensation, imagination and circumstance shape a response.

Four foundations

A practice built around behaviour.

01

Psychology-informed actor training

Why do two people respond differently to the same event? An inquiry into perception, memory, conditioning, bodily sensation, imagination and circumstance.

02

Improvisation inspired by screenplay writing

Wants, opposing intentions, obstacles, conflict and consequences give dramatic improvisations their structure. Behaviour is discovered as a situation unfolds.

03

Text analysis, interpretation & subtext

Decoding screenplay scenes beyond dialogue: dramatic action, relationships, unspoken intentions and the ways they register on screen.

04

Camera dynamics

Across the final three weeks, attention, thought, stillness and movement are explored in relation to the frame and the actor’s screen presence.

The four-week journey

A cumulative process.

The intensive moves from sensory awareness to dramatic improvisation, characterisation and finally screenplay scenes. Each day begins with approximately one hour of grounding, relaxation and sensory work.

01

Week one

Awareness, sensation, instinct & imagination

The actor turns attention to the body, senses and immediate environment: noticing impulses and responding to the circumstances in front of them rather than producing an emotional result.

Areas of exploration

  • Somatic awareness — grounding and centering
  • Observer’s mindset — attention without premature interpretation
  • Sense memory — sensory experience and imagination
  • Physical destination and object work
  • Imaginary circumstances
  • Behavioural Improvisation I
  • Internal monologue

02

Week two

Improvisation: want, action & reaction

Screenplay-inspired dramatic improvisation. Wants, opposing intentions and obstacles create pressure; actors discover behaviour as circumstances change.

Areas of exploration

  • Behavioural Improvisation II — moral dilemmas
  • Want improvisation — internal and external wants
  • Relationships and stakes
  • Status dynamics — power and hierarchy
  • Conflict and changing strategies

03

Week three

Characterisation: discovering possibilities within

Through imagined histories, relationships and social circumstances, the actor explores unfamiliar possibilities within themselves—not an external imitation. Habits, contradictions and physical choices become part of the inquiry.

Areas of exploration

  • Neutrality — recognising habitual patterns
  • Body-led characterisation
  • Animal study — rhythm and instinct
  • Mask work
  • Newsroom exercise
  • Character backstory
  • Behavioural possibilities — contradictions and unexpected choices

04

Week four

Text, subtext & screen performance

Monologues and screenplay scenes bring the earlier explorations into contact with written material. The script becomes a field of action, not a set of lines to illustrate.

Areas of exploration

  • Text analysis — decoding dramatic circumstances and structure
  • Text and subtext
  • Action through language
  • Monologue work
  • Scene work — conflict and shifting intentions

Parallel strand / Weeks 2–4

Camera dynamics.

Across Weeks 2–4, Camera Dynamics explores presence within the frame: how attention, thought, stillness and movement shape what the camera reveals. The actor stays connected to the inner process while understanding the demands of a shot.

Areas include shot sizes, physical space, doing rather than demonstrating, camera position and continuity of inner life across repeated takes.

From exercise to dramatic situation

Each stage informs the next.

Sensory work sharpens observation; screenplay-inspired improvisation places that awareness under dramatic pressure; characterisation opens unfamiliar behavioural possibilities. Text analysis then brings circumstances, relationships, objectives, turning points and subtext into performance.
